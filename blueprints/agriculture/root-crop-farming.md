                ## Purpose
                Dasheen, cassava, sweet potato, eddoes and yams differ in how long they hold the land, how much water they want and who buys them. Sweet potato can be lifted in three to five months while cassava and yams may hold a field for most of a year, so the first choice decides your cash flow as much as your workload. Picking one quick crop and one slower crop for the first season keeps money coming in while you learn the longer one.

                3. One quick crop and at most one slow crop chosen for the first season, with the reason written down.
                4. The area to be planted to each crop set in beds or fractions of an acre.

                A crop that grows well but has no steady buyer is a hobby. Ask buyers before you decide, not after you harvest.

            - name: Field walk to match roots to ground
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Because roots grow underground, the soil decides the crop more than for almost anything else you could plant. Cassava, yams and sweet potato rot in ground that holds water, while dasheen will grow in wet, low-lying land that would drown them. Walking the field after heavy rain and digging a few test holes shows which parts suit which root before you spend money preparing it.

                ## Milestones
                1. The field walked within a day of heavy rain, with standing water and slow-draining patches marked on a sketch.
                2. Four or more holes dug to a spade's depth and beyond, with stones, hardpan or clay noted for each.
                3. Each part of the field labelled as suited to dasheen, to the drier-ground roots, or to neither.
                4. A soil sample sent for testing, or a date booked with the extension officer to take one.

                ## Notes
                Stony or compacted ground gives forked, twisted cassava and sweet potato that buyers mark down. Shallow ground is often better given to dasheen or kept for a later season.

            - name: Sourcing clean planting material
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Every root crop is grown from a piece of the last crop: cassava stakes, sweet potato vine cuttings, dasheen heads and suckers, eddoe cormels and yam setts. Pests and diseases travel in that material, so planting a neighbour's weevil-damaged vines or virus-streaked cassava stakes can ruin a field before it starts. Finding a clean source, ideally a government propagation station or a grower with a known record, is the single best start a root crop field can get.

                ## Milestones
                1. The extension office and any government propagation station asked what planting material they supply and when.
                2. At least two private sources visited, with the parent crop inspected for leaf spots, weevil holes and mosaic.
                3. Enough material for the first planting booked, with the variety name and source written down.
                4. A plan to hold back clean material from your own first crop for the next planting.

                ## Notes
                Cassava stakes from mature stems keep for a short time only once cut, and sweet potato vines are best planted within a few days. Collect close to planting day.
              priority: high

            - name: Weed control through the first ninety days
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Young root crops lose most yield to weeds in the first two to three months, before the canopy closes and shades the ground. Cassava and yams are slow to cover, so a field left unweeded through the early rains can lose a large share of its harvest. A regular weeding rhythm, by hand, hoe or mulch, during that window does more for yield than almost anything bought from a shop.

                ## Milestones
                1. The critical weeding window for each planting marked in the field book from its planting date.
                2. A method chosen for each block: hand weeding, hoeing, mulching or a combination.
                3. Labour or your own hours set aside for weeding each week of the window.
                4. Every block weeded at least every two to three weeks until the canopy closes.

                ## Notes
                Hoeing too close to swelling sweet potatoes and yams cuts roots. Work shallow near the plant and deeper between rows. Any herbicide use belongs with the label and the extension officer, not guesswork.
              priority: medium

            - name: Sweet versus bitter cassava and cyanide safety
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Cassava contains compounds that release cyanide, and bitter varieties contain far more than sweet ones. Bitter cassava is safe only after proper processing, which is why it goes to processors for farine, cassava bread and starch rather than to the fresh market. # Root Crop Farming

This area is for anyone growing dasheen, cassava, sweet potato, eddoes or yams to sell, from a part-time grower with a quarter acre behind the house to a farmer planting cassava by the acre for a processor. It starts with foundations (which roots to grow, the right field, a planting calendar for wet and dry seasons, clean planting material, land preparation and buyers lined up before the first stake goes in), then the weekly and monthly machinery of field walks, weeding, rain records, staggered plantings, storage checks and price watching, the crop knowledge that separates good yields from poor ones, decisions on varieties, harvesting and where to sell, dated events such as the first harvest and the Christmas market, situations from family land to hillside plots, and finally specialist work on yam barns, selling planting material and dual dasheen harvests.

What repeats is a Saturday field walk, a Tuesday weeding check in the early weeks, a daily rain gauge reading, a Wednesday price check, a Friday look at stored roots, a monthly planting batch, records and sales tally, and quarterly and yearly reviews.
