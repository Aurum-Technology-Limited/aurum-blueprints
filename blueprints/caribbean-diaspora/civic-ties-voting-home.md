            - name: Which elections you can vote in on both sides
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Before anything else, settle which elections you are entitled to vote in: your home country's national and local elections, and any in the country where you now live. Most Commonwealth Caribbean countries have no postal or overseas voting, so a vote back home usually means being registered and physically present, often with a residency condition attached. The UK lets qualifying Commonwealth citizens vote, while the US and Canada limit voting to their own citizens, and voting where you are not entitled can carry serious immigration consequences.

                ## Milestones
                1. Your home country's rules on who may register, residency periods and overseas voting, taken from the electoral office's own website and dated.
                2. The rules for the country you live in noted for national and local elections, from its official electoral body.
                3. A one-page answer listing each election you can vote in, where and how.
                4. Any grey area, such as long absence or a second citizenship, written as a question for the electoral office.

                ## Notes
                Haitian, Surinamese and Dutch Caribbean rules differ from the Commonwealth pattern, and a Dutch passport may open options for Dutch elections that the island's own vote does not. Trust only official sources and note the date you read them.

                ## Purpose
                Many people who left years ago assume their name is still on the list, then find at election time that it was removed after a confirmation exercise or a long absence, or that it sits in a constituency they no longer have any tie to.                 ## Milestones

                4. A clear answer recorded on whether you can stay registered while living abroad.

                ## Notes
                If the list can only be checked in person, ask a trusted relative to look for you, but never hand anyone your ID card or sign forms you have not read.

# Civic Ties and Voting Back Home

This area is for anyone from the Caribbean living abroad who wants to stay a citizen in practice and not only on paper, whether that means voting on a trip home or sitting on a diaspora council. It starts with the foundations (which elections you can vote in, your registration and ID card, a civic documents folder, your representative, your consulate and your government's diaspora unit), sets up the routines that keep you informed, builds knowledge of how government, elections and budgets work back home, then moves through decisions, election season and other events, situations from the first year abroad to returning home, and specialist roles such as observing elections or standing for office.

What repeats is a monthly politics briefing, monthly checks of bills, consultations and consulate notices, a monthly word from relatives on the ground, quarterly reviews of the election calendar and your contact log, and yearly checks of your registration, ID card, the budget and the honours nomination window.
