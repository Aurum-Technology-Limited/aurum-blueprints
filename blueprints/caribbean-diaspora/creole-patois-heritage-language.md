            - name: Identifying the creole your family speaks
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Caribbean creoles are separate languages, not one: Jamaican Patwa, Trinidadian and Tobagonian Creole, Bajan, Guyanese Creolese and Belizean Kriol take most of their words from English, St Lucian and Dominican Kwéyòl from French, Papiamentu from Portuguese and Spanish, and Sranan Tongo from English with a heavy Dutch layer. Knowing exactly which one your family speaks, and from which island, parish or village, decides which dictionaries, courses and speakers will actually help. Families often call several of these simply 'patois' (from the French for local speech), so the label used at home can hide more than it shows.

                ## Milestones
                1. The language named precisely, with the island and district each grandparent grew up in.
                2. Its main source language recorded: English, French, Dutch, Portuguese or Spanish based.
                3. Two or three features that mark your family's variety noted, such as a word or sound friends from other islands comment on.
                4. Any second heritage language in the family flagged, such as Bhojpuri, Hakka Chinese or Spanish.

                ## Notes
                If the family speaks Haitian Kreyòl, the Haitian Kreyol and Heritage area is built for it, though much of this one still applies.

            - name: Recording sessions with the oldest speakers
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                The deepest form of a creole, full of words younger relatives no longer use, often lives only in people now in their eighties and nineties. Recording relaxed conversations with them about food, work, childhood and old remedies captures pronunciation, idioms and turns of phrase that no dictionary holds. Every family has a story about the recording that was never made, so start with the oldest speaker first.

                ## Milestones
                1. A list of the three oldest fluent speakers in the family or community, with how to reach each.
                2. Consent asked plainly, including whether the recording may be shared with the wider family.
                3. At least three conversations of thirty minutes or more recorded with clear sound.
                4. Each file named with speaker, place and date, and backed up in two places.
                5. A short list of unfamiliar words from each recording noted for later.

                ## Notes
                A phone placed close to the speaker on a soft cloth records better than an expensive microphone across the room. Let them talk about what they enjoy; the vocabulary comes with the stories.

            - name: Choosing a spelling system for your creole
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Most creole speakers were never taught to write their language, so the same word turns up as 'pickney', 'pikni' and 'pikny' depending on who typed it. Several languages now have a recognised system: the Cassidy-JLU spelling used by the Jamaican Language Unit at the University of the West Indies, the standard Kwéyòl orthography used in St Lucia and Dominica, the two Papiamentu spellings of Aruba and of Curaçao and Bonaire, and the Belize Kriol spelling. Picking one now keeps your word book consistent and lets you read published material.

                ## Milestones
                1. The recognised spelling systems for your creole, if any, identified.
                2. One system chosen for your word book, with the reason noted.
                3. A one-page key of how its letters map to sounds kept at the front of the word book.
                4. Ten familiar words rewritten in the chosen system.

                ## Notes
                Popular spelling on social media is fine for chatting with friends. Use the standard system where you want consistency or want to read published texts.
              priority: high

              frontmatter:

            - name: Tense and aspect markers
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Creoles mark time with small words before the verb rather than endings on it, and this is where learners give themselves away. Jamaican uses 'a' for what is happening ('mi a nyam') and 'did' or 'en' for the past, Trinidadian and Bajan use 'does' for habits, St Lucian Kwéyòl uses 'ka', 'té' and 'kay', and Papiamentu uses 'ta', 'a' and 'lo'. Getting these right does more for sounding natural than any amount of vocabulary.

                ## Milestones
                1. The markers for present, past, future and habit in your creole written in a table with examples.
                2. Twenty sentences of your own written using each marker and checked by a speaker.
                3. Negation, such as 'nuh', 'eh' or 'pa', added to the table.
                4. The markers used correctly through a ten-minute conversation.
              priority: medium
              frontmatter:

                2. Three short published texts read aloud in the chosen spelling.

# Creole and Patois Heritage Language

This area is for anyone keeping a Caribbean creole alive, whether Jamaican Patwa, Trinidadian, Bajan, Guyanese Creolese, Belizean Kriol, St Lucian and Dominican Kwéyòl, Papiamentu or Sranan Tongo, and especially for parents raising children abroad who should hear it. It starts with foundations (naming the language, an honest baseline, recording the oldest speakers and choosing a spelling), moves through the routines that keep it spoken, the sounds, grammar, history, proverbs and songs worth learning, then decisions about courses, family strategy and school, and events from a creole-only day back home to a storytelling night. It ends with situations such as a toddler, a teenager who answers in English or an elder living with dementia, then specialist work on transcription, writing, teaching, archives and endangered varieties.

What repeats is daily listening and bedtime songs, a weekly call with an elder, twice-weekly word reviews, a monthly conversation group, quarterly recordings of your own speech and a yearly review around a creole day. The Habit tracker, Course, Reading queue and Content pipeline templates pair with the projects that name them. Installing adds all 50 projects as active, so archive the ones that are not for you yet.
