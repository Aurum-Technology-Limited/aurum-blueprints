                ## Purpose
                Ask five relatives where the family land is, how big it is and whose name is on it, and you may get five answers. A single page with the parcel's district, rough size, any deed or title number, who lives on it and what has been paid recently gives everyone the same starting point and shows at once what is still unknown.

              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                When land passes without a will, the people with a share are fixed by law and by who has died since, not by who lives closest or cares most. Drawing the line of heirs from the person named on the deed down to today's living relatives shows how many people must be found, consulted and eventually sign, which is often far more than the family expects.

                ## Milestones
                1. The registered or deeded owner identified as the starting point.

                4. Unclear branches, such as children born outside marriage or relatives the family lost touch with, flagged for the lawyer.
                5. The map reviewed by your lawyer, with the branches that carry a share confirmed.

                ## Notes
                This is a legal map, not a family tree for its own sake. Who counts as an heir depends on your home country's succession law and the dates of each death, so treat your draft as a question for the lawyer, not an answer.

                Whether the land is registered under a title system or held under older unregistered deeds changes everything that follows, from how the estate is administered to what a buyer will accept. A search at the land registry, done by your lawyer or a title clerk, shows whose name the land is really in, its size on record and any mortgage, caveat or charge against it.

# Family Land and Succession Back Home

Family land back home is often the most valuable and least documented thing a Caribbean family owns: held in a dead grandparent's name, lived on by one branch and claimed in conversation by everyone. This area starts with the foundations (a fact sheet, the old papers, a map of the heirs, a title search and a lawyer), then builds the routines that keep the land safe, the knowledge of titles, intestacy and probate, the big steps of administration, transfer, survey, buyout and sale, the dated moments such as signing day and mediation, situations from the relative living on the land to the heir who has never seen it, and the specialist work of untangling titles that have sat untouched for generations.

The rhythm is monthly and yearly: a fund contribution, a caretaker call and a lawyer chase each month, a quarterly update to every heir and a quarterly look at the risk register, and the land tax, insurance, bushing and agreement review each year.
