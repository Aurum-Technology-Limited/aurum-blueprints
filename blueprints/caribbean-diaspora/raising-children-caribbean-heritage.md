            - name: What we want our children to carry from home
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Most parents abroad hope their children will know where they come from, but few have written down what that means in practice: which islands, which foods, which faith, which words, which values. A one-page heritage statement listing what you most want each child to carry by eighteen turns a vague wish into a short list you can act on. It also shows the gaps, such as a grandmother's Guyanese side that nobody talks about.

                ## Milestones
                1. Every heritage the children inherit listed, including both parents' islands and any African, Indian, Chinese, Portuguese, Syrian or European roots.
                2. Five to eight things written down that you most want each child to know, do or feel by eighteen.
                3. Two or three things you are content to let go of, written down so they stop causing guilt.
                4. The statement saved or printed where both parents can find it.

                ## Notes
                Keep it to one page. A statement your child could read at twelve without rolling their eyes is about the right length.

# Raising Children with Caribbean Heritage

This area is for parents in London, Birmingham, Toronto, Brooklyn or Miami raising children whose roots are in Jamaica, Trinidad and Tobago, Guyana, Barbados, Haiti, the OECS islands or anywhere else in the region, and who want those children to grow up knowing where they come from rather than hearing about it once a year. It starts with the foundations (what you want them to carry, a plan agreed with your co-parent, a family directory, tree, map and bookshelf), then the weekly and monthly routines that keep heritage in the house, the stories, songs, history and manners worth teaching, the decisions about language, classes, community and the harder conversations about race and family habits, the occasions to prepare a child for, the situations that change the approach (babies, teenagers, mixed heritage, adoption, two households) and the longer projects of a heritage book, a teen-led project and a children's club.

What repeats is a weekly call with grandparents, Sunday lunch cooked together, a folk tale night and a word of the week, monthly cards for family birthdays, cousin calls, an island of the month and a one-to-one with each teenager, a quarterly check-in between parents and a yearly refresh of the heritage calendar, tree and directory. The Reading queue, Habit tracker, Meeting notes, Purchase decision and Trip templates pair with the projects that name them. Installing adds all 50 projects as active, so archive the ones that are not for you yet.
