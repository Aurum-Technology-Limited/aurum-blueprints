            - name: Choosing which pan to start on
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Tenor (the lead pan that usually carries the melody), double second, guitar, cello and bass each ask for different hands, reach and ears, and the section you start in shapes the music you will learn for years. Trying three voices before committing means you pick the one that suits your body and the sound you love, instead of whichever pan happened to be free on the first night.

                ## Milestones
                1. At least three different pan voices tried for a few minutes each, with a teacher or band member present.
                2. A short note written on how each felt: reach, stick weight, and whether you could hear your own part.
                3. One starting voice chosen, with a reason in a sentence.
                4. The chosen section leader or teacher told, so you are placed on that pan from the next session.

                ## Notes
                Many teachers start adults on tenor or double second because the note layout is easier to see. Bass players need to move between several drums at once, which suits people who like rhythm more than melody.
              priority: high

            - name: Learning the note layout of your pan
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Every pan voice lays its notes out differently, and the tenor's circle of fourths and fifths puts neighbouring notes a musical fourth or fifth apart rather than in a row like a piano. Mapping the layout until you can find any note without looking for it is what turns hunting into playing, and it makes every tune after it faster to learn.

                ## Milestones
                1. A paper or photo diagram of your pan's note layout, labelled with note names.
                2. The pattern of fourths and fifths, or your pan's own logic, explained in your own words.
                3. Every note on the pan found and named from a random call in under five seconds.
                4. One major scale played from memory without looking at the diagram.

                ## Notes
                Layouts vary between makers and between low C and low D tenors, so label the pan you actually play, not a diagram from the internet.
              priority: medium
              frontmatter:
                mode: learning
                output_kind: knowledge
                success_criteria: "You can name and strike any note on your pan within five seconds when someone calls it out, without a diagram."
                cadence: phased
                effort_hours_estimate: "6"
              tasks:
                - "Photograph your pan from above and label every note on the print"
                - "Find the octave pairs on the pan and mark them in the same colour"
                - "Ask someone to call random notes while you find them"
                - "Play a C major scale with the diagram turned face down"

            - name: Hearing protection for rehearsals
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                A full steel orchestra rehearsing indoors, or a Panorama band with its engine room (the rhythm section of drum kit, congas, scratcher and iron) going hard, can be loud enough to damage hearing over a season. Wearing filtered musician's earplugs from the first rehearsal protects the ears you need to hear pitch, and still lets you hear your section clearly.

                ## Milestones
                1. A pair of filtered musician's earplugs bought, or custom moulds booked with a hearing professional.
                2. Earplugs worn for every full rehearsal for a month.
                3. Your rehearsal position checked so you are not standing directly beside the iron or the drum kit when there is a choice.
                4. A spare pair kept in your kit bag.

                ## Notes
                Foam plugs muffle high notes and make it hard to judge your own pitch. Filtered plugs cut volume more evenly. Ringing ears after rehearsal is a sign to protect them better, not to toughen up.
              priority: high
              deadlineOffsetDays: 14
              frontmatter:
                mode: building
                output_kind: habit
                success_criteria: "Filtered earplugs have been worn at every full rehearsal for four consecutive weeks, with a spare pair in the kit bag."
                cadence: one-shot
                effort_hours_estimate: "1"
              tasks:
                - "Order a pair of filtered musician's earplugs"
                - "Put the earplugs in your kit bag tonight"
                - "Ask the section leader whether you can stand away from the iron"
                - "Book a hearing check if your ears ring after rehearsals"
            - name: Home practice space and pan storage
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Pans dent, rust and go out of tune when they are stacked, left in hot cars, kept in damp sheds or knocked off a table by a child. A fixed corner at home with a stand, a cover and enough room to play is what makes daily practice possible, and it protects an instrument that can cost as much as a good guitar.

                ## Milestones

                2. The pan on a stable stand with a cloth cover when not in use.
                3. Neighbours or housemates asked about reasonable practice hours.

                ## Notes

            - name: Tuning checks and the tuner's yearly visit
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Even a well-made pan drifts out of tune with playing, knocks and heat, and only a skilled pan tuner should hammer a note back into pitch. Listening for sour notes each quarter and booking the tuner in good time before the busy season keeps your instrument blending with the band, and stops small drift turning into a costly repair.

                ## Milestones
                1. A pan tuner identified, with their contact details and usual fee.
                2. A quarterly check done with a tuner app, noting any note that sounds off.
                3. The tuner booked well before Panorama or the main performance season.
                4. A record kept of each tuning date and the notes adjusted.

                ## Notes
                Do not try to fix a note yourself with a hammer. A wrong strike can crack the note or spoil the ones around it.
              priority: high

# Steelpan Playing

This area is for anyone who wants to play the steelpan properly, whether you grew up hearing a band practise down the road in Port of Spain or San Fernando, or first touched a tenor at a community class in London, Brooklyn or Toronto. It starts with the foundations (which pan to play, where to find a panyard, sticks, hearing protection and a first instrument of your own), then the routines that keep you improving and the instrument in tune, the skills of learning by ear, reading, rolling and strumming, the decisions about better pans and new sections, Panorama season and other performances, plans for beginners, children, returners, players abroad and teachers, and finally arranging, solo gigs, tuning and leading a section.

What repeats is a short daily practice, a weekly scale rotation and rehearsal check, a monthly clean, stick check, recording and band dues, a quarterly tuning listen and repertoire run, and the tuner's yearly visit. The Purchase decision, Metrics log, Habit tracker, Remediation plan and Operational checklist templates pair with the first pan, practice log, daily practice, technique fix and competition night projects. Installing adds all 50 projects as active, so archive the ones that are not for you yet.
