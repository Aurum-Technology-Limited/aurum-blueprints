            - name: Honest picture of each adult child relationship
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Parents of grown-up children often run on an impression formed years ago: the eldest is fine, the youngest needs watching. Writing a short, honest page for each child, covering how often you speak, what you talk about, what goes unsaid and where it gets tense, shows you which relationships need effort and which simply need you to keep doing what works. Everything else in this area starts from that page.

                ## Milestones
                1. One page per adult child covering contact frequency, usual topics, sore subjects and the last real disagreement.
                2. Each relationship rated in a sentence as close, steady, strained or distant, with one piece of evidence for the rating.
                3. One thing you would like to be different with each child written down in your own words.
                4. The pages kept somewhere private and dated so they can be compared in a year.

                ## Notes
                Write about what happens, not what the child is like. 'We only speak when she needs something' is useful; 'she is selfish' is not, and tends to harden into a story.

            - name: Agreeing a contact rhythm with each child
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                One parent rings every day and is told they smother; another waits to be called and hears nothing for a month. Asking each child directly how often they would like to talk, by which channel and at what time of day removes the guesswork on both sides. A rhythm both of you chose is far easier to keep than one you are quietly keeping score of.

                ## Milestones
                1. Each child asked how often, how and when they like to be in touch.
                2. Your own preference stated honestly in the same conversation.
                3. An agreed rhythm per child written down, such as a Sunday call and messages in between.
                4. A plan for what either of you does when the rhythm slips, without guilt-tripping.

                ## Notes
                Expect different answers from different children. Treating them identically is less fair than matching each one's life, shifts and time zone.
              priority: high
              deadlineOffsetDays: 30
              frontmatter:
                mode: building
                output_kind: decision
                success_criteria: "A written contact rhythm agreed with every adult child, naming frequency, channel and the usual time."
                cadence: one-shot
                effort_hours_estimate: "2"
              tasks:
                - "Note how often you currently speak with each child and who initiates"
                - "Ask each child what contact rhythm suits their week"
                - "Write the agreed rhythm for each child in your calendar"
                - "Agree a light way to say the rhythm has slipped"

            - name: Same page with your partner on grown-up children
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Children quickly learn which parent says yes, and that pattern rarely stops at 18. If you and your partner, or your former partner, differ on money, housing or how much to say about a child's choices, an adult child gets mixed messages and one of you ends up the villain. A frank conversation that produces a few shared positions protects both the marriage and the relationship with each child.

                ## Milestones
                1. Each of you has written your view on money, moving back home and commenting on their partners.
                2. The points where you disagree listed without trying to settle them on the spot.
                3. Three or four shared positions agreed and written down.
                4. An agreement that neither of you says yes to a significant request without talking first.

                ## Notes
                Where parents are separated, aim only for the two or three positions that matter most, such as how money requests are handled, and agree them in writing.
              priority: high

            - name: Apologising for things you got wrong as a parent
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Many adult children carry a specific memory, a harsh punishment, a divorce handled badly, a sibling clearly favoured, that has never been acknowledged.                 3. The apology given at a calm moment chosen with their circumstances in mind.
                4. Their response accepted, including if it is silence or anger.

                ## Notes
                Leave out 'but' and 'if you felt'. If the history includes serious harm, consider talking it through with a counsellor before you speak to your child.
              priority: medium
              frontmatter:
                mode: learning
                output_kind: event-completion
                success_criteria: "A specific apology has been drafted, delivered at a calm moment, and the child's response recorded without pressing for forgiveness."
                cadence: phased
                effort_hours_estimate: "4"
              tasks:
                - "Write down what you think your child would say you got wrong"
                - "Draft an apology of five sentences or fewer with no excuses"

# Parenting Adult Children

This area is for parents whose children are now adults, whether they are 19 and back in the box room or 45 with children of their own, and for retirees working out what the relationship looks like when nobody needs a lift to football any more. It starts with foundations (an honest picture of each relationship, your own limits, a ledger of the help you already give and agreement with your partner), moves through the routines that keep you close, the listening and repair skills that matter most now, decisions about money, housing and childcare, the milestones of partners, weddings and grandchildren, versions for boomerang kids, divorce, illness and fixed incomes, and ends with the specialist work of estrangement, later-life wishes and serious debt.

What repeats is a regular call with each child, a monthly meal or video date, a weekly note of the advice you held back, a quarterly question about how your help is landing, and a yearly review of the money you give and of the relationship as a whole.
