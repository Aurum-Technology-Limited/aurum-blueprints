            - name: Agreeing your family approach to pocket money
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Children notice quickly when one parent says no and the other quietly hands over cash, and the lesson they learn is to ask the softer parent. Before any jar or amount, the adults who pay need to agree a handful of positions: whether pocket money is tied to chores, who pays for what, whether advances exist and what happens to birthday money. Thirty minutes of agreement now saves years of being played off against each other.

                ## Milestones
                1. Each parent's own childhood experience of pocket money shared and the parts worth keeping named.
                2. A short written position on chores, advances, gifts and what pocket money must cover.
                3. One line agreed on what each parent does when the other is not there and a child asks for money.
                4. Grandparents or other regular payers told the outline, so they do not undo it by accident.

                ## Notes
                Most families find it simplest to keep basic pocket money separate from everyday chores, which children do because they live in the house, and to pay only for genuinely extra jobs.

            - name: Setting a pocket money amount for each age
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                There is no correct figure, but there is a sensible process: look at what the money must cover, check what other families nearby tend to give, and set a rule that rises with age so the conversation is not reopened every week. A simple formula, such as a base amount per year of age, makes rises predictable and fair between siblings.

                ## Milestones
                1. What the money must cover written down for each child's age.
                2. Two or three published surveys or local parents consulted for typical amounts.
                3. A formula chosen that sets the amount from age, with the current figure for each child.
                4. The amount checked against the family budget for the next twelve months.

                ## Notes
                Choose an amount you can pay every single week without fail. A smaller amount paid reliably teaches far more than a generous one that is often forgotten.
              priority: high

              frontmatter:

# Pocket Money & Kids' Money Lessons

This area is for parents who want their children to handle money well long before they handle much of it. It runs from the foundations (agreeing your approach, choosing an amount, setting up jars and a written agreement) through the weekly and monthly routines that keep pocket money steady, the skills children practise from counting coins to spotting sales tricks, decisions such as savings accounts, kids' cards and in-game spending, a few memorable firsts, versions for preschoolers, pre-teens, two homes and tight budgets, and finally teenage budgets, first earnings, family loans and how investing works.

What repeats is a Saturday pay day, a Sunday look at the savings chart, a monthly jar count and interest payment, a quarterly money check-in with each child, a quarterly giving decision and a pocket money rise on each birthday. The Metrics log, Savings goal, Reading queue, Purchase decision and Monthly budget and bills templates pair with the projects that name them.
