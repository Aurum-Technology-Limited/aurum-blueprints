            - name: Safe preserving rules and trusted recipe sources
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Home preserving goes wrong in two ways: a jar that spoils visibly, which is a nuisance, and a low-acid jar that looks fine but can carry botulism, which is dangerous. Settling on two or three tested sources before the first batch, such as a national food preservation centre or a university extension service, and writing down the few rules you will never break turns every later recipe into following rather than guessing.

                ## Milestones
                1. Two or three tested recipe sources chosen and bookmarked, with the reason each one is trusted.
                2. A one-page house rules sheet covering acidity, headspace, processing and when a jar is thrown away.
                3. Old or untested methods such as open kettle canning, oven canning and wax seals listed as things this kitchen does not do.
                4. The rules sheet printed and kept with the preserving equipment.

                ## Notes
                Fermenting and canning carry different risks. Fermented vegetables rely on salt and the acidity bacteria build; canned food relies on acid plus heat. A recipe that is safe for one method is not automatically safe for the other.

                Before you start: this plan is general information to help you organise your own health. It is not medical advice and does not replace a doctor, nurse, pharmacist or other qualified professional. Check any change to treatment, medicine or diet with them first, and get urgent care if you feel unwell.

            - name: Gram scale, pH strips and thermometer
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Ferments are salted by percentage of weight, and both a jam's set and a pickle's safety depend on numbers: brine strength, boiling temperature, acidity. A scale reading single grams, a probe thermometer and pH strips or a calibrated meter let you check what recipes assume instead of trusting cups and spoonfuls.

                ## Milestones
                1. A scale that weighs in 1 gram steps, plus a decision on a 0.1 gram pocket scale for small salt and spice amounts.
                2. A probe thermometer reading to at least 110°C, checked in boiling water.
                3. Narrow-range pH strips, or a pH meter with calibration solutions, added to the kit.
                4. A first test reading written in the batch log.

                ## Notes
                Home pH testing is a good learning tool for ferments. It does not make an untested canning recipe safe, so keep using tested recipes for anything that goes on a shelf.

                ## Purpose
                Sauerkraut is the best first ferment: cabbage and salt only, a forgiving process and a clear result in two to four weeks. Weighing the cabbage, adding salt at 2 percent of its weight and keeping it under its own brine teaches the core of all vegetable fermentation in a single jar.

# Fermenting & Preserving

This area is for anyone who wants a cupboard and fridge stocked with their own preserves, from a first jar of sauerkraut to a year of marmalade, and for creative cooks who treat fermentation as a craft. It starts with the foundations (safe rules and tested sources, fermenting and canning kit, a batch log, a first kraut, quick pickle and jam), then the routines that keep crocks, cultures and shelves healthy, the skills of brines, kimchi, jam setting, chutney, sodas, vinegar, drying and chilli sauce, the decisions on equipment, sugar, temperature and method, the seasonal sessions, gifts, swaps and shows, versions for small flats, children, growers, hot kitchens and vulnerable households, and finally pressure canning, miso, selling and teaching.
