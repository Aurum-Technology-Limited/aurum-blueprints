                ## Purpose
                Nothing can be licensed until the university formally knows the invention exists, and the invention disclosure form is the document every later negotiation points back to. Filing a complete one now, with every contributor, funding source and planned publication listed, starts the formal process and stops the office discovering gaps months later while an investor is waiting.

              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Universities differ widely in what they claim and what they give back: some take equity and royalties, some offer a fixed founder-friendly deal, and student work is often treated differently from staff work. Reading your own institution's IP policy and spinout guidance before the first meeting means you negotiate from the published rules rather than from whatever the office proposes first.

                4. Three questions the policy leaves unclear written down for the technology transfer office.

                ## Notes
                A growing number of universities publish standard spinout terms after national reviews of how spinout deals are struck. If yours does, those terms are the anchor for every later conversation.

            - name: Ownership map of the underlying research IP
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Research results rarely belong to one owner: a funder may hold rights, an industry sponsor may have an option, a collaborator's university may co-own a patent, and code or data may carry open licences. Mapping who owns or has a claim on each piece now is exactly what investors' lawyers will do in due diligence, and finding a sponsor's option yourself is far cheaper than finding it during a round.

                ## Milestones
                1. Each asset listed: patents and applications, software, data sets, materials and know-how.
                2. Every grant, sponsored research and material transfer agreement checked for IP, option and publication clauses.
                3. Third-party claims and open-source licences marked against each asset.
                4. The map reviewed with the technology transfer office and every gap given an owner to resolve it.

                ## Notes
                Public and charitable funders sometimes keep rights to step in or take the IP back if it is not used, and industry sponsors often hold a time-limited right of first negotiation. Both shape what can be licensed exclusively.
