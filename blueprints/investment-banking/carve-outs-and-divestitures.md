            - name: Carve-out deal anatomy from perimeter to day one
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Selling a division looks like any sale process from the outside, but most of the risk sits in work an ordinary auction never touches: deciding what is in the perimeter, building financials that never existed, and keeping the business running after it leaves its parent. A one-page anatomy of a carve-out, laid over the familiar sale stages, tells you which separation workstream has to be ready before each buyer milestone.

                ## Milestones
                1. A one-page map that sets the separation workstreams (perimeter, financials, legal entities, people, IT, contracts, TSAs) against the sale stages.
                2. The points where separation work blocks the sale process, such as carve-out financials before the information memorandum, marked in colour.
                3. The difference between signing, legal day one and TSA exit written in two sentences each.
                4. The map corrected by a senior banker who has closed at least one carve-out.

                ## Notes
                The most common junior mistake is treating separation as the client's problem after signing. Buyers price separation risk into their bids from round one, so the sell-side adviser owns the story long before closing.

            - name: Parent company divestiture rationale memo
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Parents sell divisions for different reasons: a portfolio review that labels the unit non-core, an activist pushing for focus, a balance sheet that needs cash, or a regulator demanding a remedy. The reason decides what the client will trade off between price, speed, certainty and the shape of the remaining group, so writing it down early stops the deal team from optimising for the wrong thing.

            - name: Transaction perimeter definition
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                The perimeter is the list of what the buyer gets: legal entities, product lines, sites, employees, contracts, intellectual property and customer relationships. Divisions rarely match legal entities, so a perimeter left vague in month one turns into a renegotiation in month five, usually when the buyer finds a key patent or plant sitting outside it.

                ## Milestones
                1. A perimeter schedule listing the entities, sites, product lines and functions in scope, with each marked fully in, partly in or out.
                2. Every partly in item given an owner and a proposed treatment such as transfer, licence, shared use or TSA.
                3. Borderline items, such as a shared plant or a customer served by two divisions, escalated to the client with options.
                4. A signed-off perimeter version that the model, the information memorandum and the legal reorganisation all use.

                ## Notes
                Version the perimeter and date every change. A perimeter that differs between the model and the draft SPA is the fastest way to lose a buyer's trust.
              priority: high
              deadlineOffsetDays: 30
              frontmatter:
                mode: building
                output_kind: artifact
                success_criteria: "A dated perimeter schedule with every item classified and owned, signed off by the client and used as the single source for the model and documents."
                cadence: one-shot
                effort_hours_estimate: "10"
              tasks:
                - "Request the parent's legal entity chart and the division's management reporting structure"
                - "List every site, entity and product line and mark it in, out or partly in"
                - "Propose a treatment for each partly in item and name an owner"
                - "Take the borderline items to the client with two options each"
            - name: Shared asset and entanglement inventory
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                A division inside a large group leans on dozens of shared things: an ERP instance, a treasury cash pool, group insurance, a shared warehouse, procurement contracts, payroll, a common brand and people who split their time. Listing every entanglement before buyers ask is what lets you size the TSA, the standalone cost and the one-off separation cost with any confidence.

                ## Milestones
                1. An inventory of shared services, assets, contracts and people, grouped by function such as finance, HR, IT, procurement, legal and facilities.
                2. Each entanglement given a likely separation route: transfer, replicate, TSA, long-term agreement or leave behind.
                3. Shared people identified by name role and percentage of time spent on the division.
                4. The ten hardest entanglements ranked by cost and time to separate, with the client's functional owner for each.

                ## Notes
                Interview the functional heads, not just finance. Treasury, insurance and IT security are the entanglements most often missed because they never appear in the division's cost centre.
              priority: high

            - name: Stranded cost tracker and mitigation log
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                When a division leaves, the parent keeps costs that used to be shared with it: head office functions, IT platforms, property and corporate overhead now spread over a smaller business. # Carve-Outs and Divestitures

This area is for the banker advising a parent company on selling a division, or a buyer on acquiring one, where the hard part is not the auction but untangling a business that was never built to stand alone. It starts with foundations (the deal anatomy, the parent's rationale, the perimeter, the shared asset inventory, structure options, readiness, carve-out financials, governance and timetable), moves into the operating rhythm of steering meetings, dependency logs, TSA and stranded cost trackers, then the skills of allocations, standalone costs, working capital and TSA pricing, the separation decisions on IT, contracts, brand, pensions and tax questions, the dated moments from board approval to TSA exit, situations for each seat and for sponsor buyers, and finally specialist work: remedy divestitures, multi-country separations, distressed parents and retained stakes.

What repeats is a weekly separation steering meeting, a twice-weekly bidder concerns review while the process is live, weekly logs for dependencies, perimeter changes, people and contract consents, a monthly TSA and stranded cost review and a quarterly look at mitigation progress. The Reading queue, Meeting notes, Risk register and Operational checklist templates pair with the projects that name them. Keep client names, division numbers and live terms inside your firm's systems; these projects hold your own working method, not a client's confidential data.
