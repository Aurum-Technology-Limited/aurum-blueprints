              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Gentle slow breathing suits almost everyone, but fast, forceful or breath-holding styles can be risky with epilepsy, heart rhythm problems, uncontrolled blood pressure, pregnancy, glaucoma or a history of psychosis. A short conversation with your doctor or nurse before you start tells you which techniques are fine, which to avoid and what should make you stop, so you can practise without second guessing every new sensation.

                ## Milestones
                1. Your conditions, medicines and any past fainting or seizures listed on one page.
                2. Your doctor or nurse asked, by name, about slow breathing, breath holds and fast breathing styles.
                3. Any techniques to avoid written at the top of your practice notes.
                4. The signs that mean stop a session written down, such as chest pain, faintness or feeling detached from your surroundings.

                ## Notes
                Mild tingling or light-headedness usually means you are breathing more than you need: slow down and let the breath return to normal. Anything stronger is a reason to stop and seek advice, not to push on.

              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Most adults breathe somewhere around twelve to twenty times a minute at rest without noticing, and people under strain often breathe faster, higher in the chest and through the mouth. Counting your breaths, noticing where the movement happens and timing a comfortable pause after an outbreath gives you a starting point, so in two months you can tell whether practice is changing anything at all.

                ## Milestones
                1. Breaths per minute counted at rest on three different days.
                2. Where the breath moves, chest or belly, noted with one hand on each.
                3. Nose or mouth breathing at rest recorded.
                4. A comfortable pause after a normal outbreath timed, stopping at the first urge to breathe.
                5. All four observations written in your practice log with dates.

                ## Notes
                The pause check is about comfort, not endurance. Stop at the first clear urge to breathe and never push through it.

                3. Your pacer set to that rate.
                4. Twenty-minute sessions at that rate done weekly for a month.

                ## Notes
                If slower rates make you light-headed or air hungry, breathe more gently rather than more deeply. The rate matters more than the size of each breath.

# Breathwork & Nervous System Regulation

This area is for anyone who wants their breath and body to work as a brake when stress builds, whether in a tense meeting, after a near miss on the road or at the end of a long day. It starts with the foundations (a safety check, a baseline of how you breathe now and one go-to calming breath), then the daily and weekly routines that make regulation automatic, the specific techniques worth learning, decisions about teachers, devices and online trends, the occasions worth preparing for, variants for parents, pregnancy, lung conditions, shift workers, older adults and carers, and finally the work of an experienced practitioner.

What repeats is a daily five-minute session, a weekday downshift after work, paced walks and humming practice through the week, a Sunday look at the log, a monthly toolkit and playbook check, a quarterly new technique trial and an annual review.
