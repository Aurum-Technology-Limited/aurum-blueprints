            - name: Basic care floor for the first fortnight
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                In the first two weeks after a death, meals, sleep and water quietly fall away while the days fill with visitors, calls and arrangements. Setting a deliberately low floor, one proper meal, a glass of water by the bed and ten minutes outside, keeps your body going through the shock without asking you to feel any particular way.

                ## Milestones
                1. Three daily basics written on a card and kept somewhere you look every morning.
                2. One person asked to check each evening that the basics happened.
                3. Fourteen days passed with at least one proper meal on most of them.
                4. Any basic that slipped badly, such as several nights without sleep, reported to your doctor.

                ## Notes
                Shock often makes people feel numb, calm or oddly busy at first. That is common and says nothing about how much you loved them.

                Before you start: this plan is general information to help you look after your own wellbeing. It is not medical or psychological advice and does not replace a doctor, therapist or counsellor. If you are in crisis, in danger or thinking about harming yourself, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line now.
              priority: high
              deadlineOffsetDays: 14
              frontmatter:
                mode: building
                output_kind: habit
                success_criteria: "Three written daily basics were met on at least ten of the first fourteen days, with one named person checking in each evening."
                cadence: phased

                - "Ask one person to text each evening to check you have eaten"

              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Most people say 'let me know if you need anything' in the first weeks, then life carries them on and the calls stop around month three, just as grief often gets heavier. Choosing three to five people on purpose, and telling each one what kind of support you would value from them, means someone is still there when the cards have stopped arriving.

                ## Milestones
                1. Three to five supporters named, each with the kind of help they are best at.

                4. The circle still in contact six months after the death.

                ## Notes
                Different people suit different needs: one who will sit with tears, one who will talk about anything else, one who will just turn up with soup.

            - name: Balancing loss time and restoration time
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Bereavement researchers describe healthy grieving as moving back and forth between facing the loss and getting on with ordinary life, rather than doing only one. Planning a little of each into the week, time to look at photos or visit the grave and time for work, friends and new tasks, stops grief either taking over every hour or being pushed down until it bursts out.

                ## Milestones
                1. The idea of oscillating between loss and restoration understood in your own words.
                2. One protected slot a week for remembering, crying or visiting.
                3. One regular slot a week for something ordinary or new.
                4. After a month, a note on whether the balance tipped too far either way.

                ## Notes
                The model is called the dual process model of coping with bereavement. People who avoid all reminders and people who think of nothing else both tend to struggle more.

# Grief & Bereavement Support

Grief is not a problem to solve or a set of stages to pass through; it is what love does when the person is gone, and it reshapes itself over months and years. This area starts with the foundations for the first weeks (a basic care floor, a small circle of supporters, a doctor's appointment, leave from work and a list of numbers for bad nights), then the gentle routines that carry you through, the skills that make grief easier to live alongside, the choices about counselling, belongings and traditions, the first birthdays, anniversaries and festive seasons, the versions for a parent, a child, a baby or an unrecognised loss, and finally the specialist support for suicide bereavement, inquests and grief that will not ease.

What repeats is deliberately light: a daily anchor routine, a Sunday evening check-in and a midweek balance of loss time and ordinary life, a monthly wellbeing review, support group and family call, and a yearly sweep of the dates that will be hard. The Habit tracker, Metrics log, Reading queue, Meeting notes and Trip templates pair with the projects that keep a routine, a log, a reading list, session notes or a return visit.
