            - name: First written crisis safety plan
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                A safety plan is a short written document, made on a calmer day, that tells you what to do step by step when thoughts of suicide or a mental health crisis arrive. Clinicians widely use a six-part format: warning signs, things you can do alone, people and places for distraction, people to ask for help, professionals and crisis lines, and making your surroundings safer. A rough first version on one page this week is worth far more than a perfect one you meant to write.

                ## Milestones
                1. A one-page plan with all six sections filled in, even briefly.
                2. The plan written in your own words rather than copied from a leaflet.
                3. A copy kept somewhere you can reach within a minute.
                4. A date set to improve the plan with someone you trust or a clinician.

                ## Notes
                If you are in danger right now, skip the planning and call your local emergency number or a crisis line. The plan is for preparing on calmer days, not for getting through this one alone.
              priority: high

              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                In a crisis, searching for a number is one more obstacle at the worst possible moment. Saving your local emergency number, a 24-hour crisis line, a text or chat service, the out-of-hours doctor and any crisis team number in your phone and on paper takes half an hour and removes that obstacle for good. Including a text option matters, because many people find speaking aloud impossible when they are at their lowest.

                3. A paper copy in your wallet or bag.

                Putting time and distance between a person and the means of harming themselves is one of the best-supported parts of safety planning, because the most intense moments of a crisis often pass. # Mental Health Crisis Safety Plan

This area is for anyone who wants a written plan for the hardest moments, and for the carers, partners and friends who are named in one. It starts with the foundations of a safety plan (warning signs, coping steps, safe people, crisis numbers and a safer home), then the routines that keep the plan current, the skills that make it usable under pressure, decisions such as an advance statement and consent to share, the dated events around hospital stays and changes of service, plans for living alone, parenting a struggling teenager or supporting someone from far away, and finally the specialist work of coordinating a whole care network.

What repeats is a weekly warning sign check, a monthly read-through and medicine check, a quarterly confirmation with your safe people and a look in the crisis bag, and a yearly rewrite with your clinician, with a daily check-in text for anyone who lives alone. The Person, Habit tracker, Operational checklist, Meeting notes and Trip templates pair with the projects that name them.
