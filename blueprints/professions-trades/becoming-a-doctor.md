            - name: Testing your reasons for choosing medicine
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Wanting to help people, liking biology or being told you are clever enough are common starting points, but none of them on its own carries someone through six or more years of study and long nights on call. Writing down why you want medicine, then testing each reason against what doctors actually do all day, gives you an honest answer before money and years are committed, and it becomes the raw material for your personal statement and interview.

                ## Milestones
                1. A page listing your reasons for medicine, each with one real experience behind it.
                2. Conversations held with at least two working doctors about the parts of the job they find hardest.
                3. Two neighbouring careers, such as nursing, physiotherapy or biomedical science, compared honestly with medicine.
                4. A written decision to pursue medicine, pause or explore an alternative, dated and kept.

                ## Notes
                Admissions panels can tell a borrowed reason from a lived one. Keep the page in plain words; polish comes later.
              priority: medium

                ## Purpose
                Medical schools set minimum subjects and grades, and a choice made at fourteen can quietly close the door at seventeen. Most schools expect Chemistry and Biology at an advanced level (CAPE Units 1 and 2, A-level or an equivalent), with Physics or Mathematics often recommended and English required at CSEC or GCSE. Checking the entry rules of real target schools before choosing subjects avoids discovering a gap after the timetable is fixed.

                ## Milestones
                1. Entry requirements of at least four target medical schools copied into one table.
                2. Subjects and minimum grades that every target school shares identified.
                3. CSEC or GCSE subjects confirmed to include English, Mathematics and the sciences those schools want.
                4. CAPE units, A-levels or equivalents chosen and registered with Chemistry and Biology included.

                ## Notes
                Requirements change. Read each school's current admissions page rather than a forum post, and check whether combined or double-award science is accepted.

                Admissions panels want proof that you know what medicine looks like up close, and the only real proof is time spent watching it. A few days shadowing in a hospital ward, a health centre or a general practice, with notes taken each day, gives you specific stories to tell and an honest test of whether the work suits you.

            - name: Admissions test preparation
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Many medical schools ask for an aptitude or knowledge test as well as grades: the UCAT in the UK, the MCAT in the US and Canada, and school-set tests at some Caribbean and offshore schools. These tests reward practice under time pressure more than raw knowledge, so a planned run of timed sections over several months moves the score far more than a cram in the final week.

                ## Milestones
                1. The tests required by each target school confirmed and a test date booked.
                2. A diagnostic practice test taken under timed conditions and the score recorded.
                3. A weekly practice schedule followed for at least eight weeks.
                4. Two full timed mock tests completed in the final fortnight.
                5. The test sat and the score sent to every school that needs it.

                ## Notes
                Check each test's official site for the current format, registration window and any fee support; do not rely on last year's details.
              priority: high
              deadlineOffsetDays: 120
              frontmatter:

                output_kind: knowledge

# Becoming a Doctor

This area is for anyone thinking seriously about medicine, from a fourth-form student weighing CSEC sciences to a graduate considering a second degree, and for the doctors in training who are already on the road. It starts with the foundations (testing your reasons, subject choices, admissions tests, funding and the application itself), then the routines that carry you through medical school and the early years, the clinical and academic skills, the decisions about schools, electives, posts and specialty, the dated milestones from interview day to the first week as an intern, the situations that change the route, and finally the work of a senior doctor.

What repeats is a weekly study plan with daily flashcard reviews, a weekly logbook entry, monthly reflection, money and journal club slots, quarterly mentor meetings, and the yearly round of registration, indemnity and continuing professional development. The Monthly budget and bills, Development plan, Job application, Purchase decision, Skills matrix and Trip templates pair with the projects that name them. Installing adds all 50 projects as active, so archive the ones that are not for you yet.
