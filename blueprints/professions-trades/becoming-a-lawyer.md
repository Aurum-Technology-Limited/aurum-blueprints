              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Television shows trials; most lawyers spend their days reading, drafting, negotiating and recording time, and many never stand up in court. Before committing years and money, talk to three practising lawyers in different settings and read about a normal working week, so the decision to study law is based on the real job rather than its image.

                ## Milestones
                1. Three conversations held with lawyers in different settings, such as a small general practice, a large firm or chambers, and government or in-house.
                2. A one-page note of what each lawyer does on an ordinary Tuesday, what they enjoy and what wears them down.
                3. An honest list of the parts of the work you would like and the parts you would dread.
                4. A written decision to pursue law, test it further, or look at a related career.

                ## Notes
                Ask about the first five years specifically. Senior lawyers often describe the job they have now, not the one you would start in.

            - name: Mapping the qualifying route where you want to practise
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Each jurisdiction qualifies lawyers differently. Across much of the Commonwealth Caribbean a law degree is followed by two years at a regional law school for the Legal Education Certificate; England and Wales uses the SQE and qualifying work experience for solicitors and the bar course and pupillage for barristers; the US requires a JD and a state bar exam; Canada adds licensing exams and articling. Choosing the jurisdiction first decides which degree, which tests and how many years.

                ## Milestones
                1. The jurisdiction or two where you most want to practise written down, with the reason.
                2. The official stages for each, from degree to admission, listed from the regulator's own website.
                3. Approximate years and the major exams for each route set side by side.
                4. Any rules on recognition between your chosen jurisdictions noted, such as whether a UK degree gives direct entry to a Caribbean law school.

                ## Notes
                Rules change, so use the current pages of the Council of Legal Education, the law society or bar council, or the state bar, not a forum post from five years ago.

# Becoming a Lawyer

Becoming a lawyer takes five to seven years of study and supervised training, and the route depends heavily on where you want to practise: the Caribbean Legal Education Certificate, the UK solicitor and barrister routes, the US JD and bar exam, or Canadian licensing. This area is for school leavers, law students, career changers and new practitioners, and it runs from a first reality check and school subjects, through admissions and the law degree, work experience and applications, law school or the bar course, admission to practice, and the first years in a firm or chambers, ending with choosing a field, setting up on your own and the long road to partnership, silk or the bench.

The recurring rhythms are a weekly study block and application review while you are a student, daily question practice in the run-up to the bar or licensing exam, and once in practice a daily check of the court diary, daily time recording, monthly supervision, quarterly development hours and the yearly practising certificate renewal.
