            - name: Testing whether policing suits you
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Police work is shift work, paperwork, court dates and long stretches of routine broken by moments when people are hurt, angry or frightened, and recruitment campaigns rarely show the paperwork. Spending a few weeks finding out what officers in your country actually do, before you spend money on applications and fitness coaching, gives you an honest yes or no. It also shows you which part of the work pulls you, from community beats to investigations.

                ## Milestones
                1. Conversations held with at least two serving or retired officers about their hardest shifts and their best ones.
                2. A station open day, community meeting or public court session attended in person.
                3. A page listing what attracts you, what worries you and what your family thinks.
                4. A dated decision to apply, wait a year or look at another route.

                ## Notes
                Ask officers about the parts nobody films: night shifts, statement writing, waiting at hospital with a prisoner, giving evidence. If those still sound bearable, the rest is learnable.
              priority: medium
              frontmatter:

                output_kind: decision
                success_criteria: "A written decision to apply, wait or drop policing, backed by two conversations with officers and one in-person visit to a station, court or community meeting."
                cadence: one-shot
                effort_hours_estimate: "8"
              tasks:
                - "Write down in fifteen minutes why policing appeals to you and what worries you"
                - "Ask two officers you know, or a community liaison officer, for a short conversation"
                - "Sit in the public gallery of a magistrates' court for one morning"
                - "Record your decision and the reason that tipped it"
            - name: Entry requirements for the service you want to join
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Every police service sets its own rules on age, nationality or residency, minimum education, height in some forces, eyesight, tattoos and criminal record, and they change between recruitment rounds. Copying the current requirements from the official recruitment page into one checklist, and ticking yourself against each line, tells you whether you can apply this round or what you must fix first. It also protects you from paying anyone who claims to sell a place.

                ## Milestones

                2. Each requirement marked met, not yet met or unclear.
                3. Every unclear line answered by the recruitment office in writing or by phone, with a note of who answered.
                4. A list of gaps, each with a date by which you will close it.

                ## Notes
                Recruitment is free in legitimate services. Anyone asking for money to guarantee a place, a faster medical or a pass mark is committing fraud and should be reported.
              priority: high

            - name: School subjects and qualifications for police entry
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Most Caribbean services ask for several CSEC passes including English A and Mathematics, and some recruit graduates or CAPE holders into faster routes. In the UK the equivalents are GCSEs and Level 3 qualifications for the degree apprenticeship, in the US a high school diploma and often college credits, and in Canada a grade 12 diploma. Getting the right passes at sixteen or seventeen, or planning resits as an adult, keeps the door open for the round you want to apply in.

                ## Milestones
                1. The academic requirement for your chosen service written down, including any minimum grades.
                2. Your current certificates or predicted grades checked against it.
                3. Useful extras identified, such as CAPE Law, Sociology or Criminology modules, Social Studies or a second language.
                4. A plan for each missing pass, such as a January resit, an evening class or a private candidate entry.

                ## Notes
                English A matters more than most applicants expect: officers write statements and reports every shift, and the written exam usually tests comprehension and composition.
              priority: medium

# Joining the Police Service

This area is for anyone thinking about a career in policing, whether a fifth-form student choosing CSEC subjects, a school leaver waiting for the next recruitment drive, a security guard or soldier changing direction at thirty, or a probationer trying to get through the first year in one piece. It starts with the foundations (finding out whether the work suits you, the entry requirements, your record, your fitness and your money), then the routines that carry you through recruitment and the early years, the knowledge the exams and the academy expect, the decisions about where and how to apply, the dated steps from fitness test to confirmation, the situations that change the route, including joining a service abroad, and finally the specialist and promotion work of an experienced officer.

What repeats is three training runs a week with a monthly fitness retest, a weekly session each on current affairs, written English and situational judgement, a monthly check of recruitment notices, and, once you are in, daily pocketbook checks, weekly kit checks and tutor meetings, monthly wellbeing check-ins and a professional development log, and a yearly review of your first aid certificate and welfare cover. The Metrics log, Monthly budget and bills, Training program, Job application, Sleep review and Development plan templates pair with the projects that name them. Installing adds all 50 projects as active, so archive the ones that are not for you yet.
