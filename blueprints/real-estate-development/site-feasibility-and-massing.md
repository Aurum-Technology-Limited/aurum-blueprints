                ## Purpose
                Feasibility can mean a ten-minute look at a map or a six-week design study, and paying for the second on a site that the first would have killed wastes money. Writing down what each stage answers, what it costs and what must be true to move on means every site gets the cheapest test that could rule it out first. Set the gates before the next site arrives, so enthusiasm for a particular plot does not skip them.

                ## Milestones
                1. Three stages defined: desktop screen, measured study and design-led feasibility, each with the questions it answers.
                2. A budget in hours and fees set for each stage.
                3. Pass and fail conditions written for moving from one stage to the next.
                4. The gates tested on one current or recent site and adjusted where they felt wrong.

                ## Notes
                Most sites should die at the desktop stage. If more than a third reach design-led feasibility, the first gate is too loose.

# Site Feasibility and Massing

Site feasibility is the work between spotting a site and offering for it: drawing the real boundary, finding what stops you building, reading the policy limits on density and height, and sketching enough massing to know whether the numbers in the appraisal have any physical basis. This area is for founders and small developers testing their own sites, builders and landowners looking at their first scheme, and small practices that sell feasibility studies. It starts with the foundations of scope, boundary, constraints and policy, moves to the routines that keep a pipeline of sites tested the same way, then the skills of reading daylight, fire, access and ground conditions, the option studies and decisions, the dated moments before an offer or a lender meeting, situations for different kinds of developer, and specialist work on rights of light, upward extensions and large masterplans.

The rhythms are a weekly screening session and pipeline review, a monthly look at the constraints register, nearby consents and policy changes, a quarterly refresh of feasibility cost rates and consultant contacts, and a yearly lookback comparing what feasibility promised with what was consented. Operational checklist suits the site visit, Purchase decision suits choosing massing software, Risk register suits the constraints register, Metrics log suits the density benchmarks and Meeting notes suits the design team site walk. Nothing here is planning, legal or engineering advice: confirm capacity with an architect and planning consultant, and legal or technical questions with the right professional.
