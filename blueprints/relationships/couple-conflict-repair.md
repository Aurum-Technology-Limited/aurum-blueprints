            - name: Mapping your last three arguments
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Most couples have far fewer different fights than it feels: the same two or three themes, started in the same way, at the same time of day. Writing down your last three arguments separately, then comparing notes calmly, shows what actually sets you off and gives every later project in this area something real to work on.

                ## Milestones
                1. Each partner's private notes on the last three arguments: the trigger, the time, where you were and how it ended.
                2. The two sets of notes compared on a calm day, with differences in memory treated as information, not a new fight.
                3. One or two recurring themes named in plain words both of you accept.
                4. The most common time and place for arguments written down.

                ## Notes
                Describe what happened, not who was to blame. If comparing notes starts heating up, stop and come back to it after you have agreed a time-out signal.
              priority: high

            - name: Time-out signal and agreed return time
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Calling a break in the middle of a row only works if both of you trust that the other will come back. A pre-agreed signal, a minimum break of twenty minutes and a promise to return at a named time turns walking away from slamming a door into a way of protecting the conversation.

                ## Milestones
                1. A word or hand gesture chosen that either partner can use to pause an argument.
                2. A minimum break of twenty minutes and a maximum of twenty-four hours agreed.
                3. A rule that whoever calls the time-out also names when the talk will resume.
                4. The signal used at least once in a real disagreement and the return honoured.

                ## Notes
                A time-out is not a way to win by leaving. If one of you uses it to end every hard conversation, that is a separate pattern worth working on with the pursue and withdraw project.
              priority: high
              deadlineOffsetDays: 14
              frontmatter:
                mode: building
                output_kind: decision
                success_criteria: "A time-out signal, a break length and a return rule are written down and have been used once with the return honoured."
                cadence: one-shot
                effort_hours_estimate: "1"
              tasks:
                - "Suggest a time-out word to your partner on a calm day"

            - name: Recognising your own flooding signs
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Once your heart is racing and your body is in fight or flight, the thinking part of the brain goes quiet and nothing useful gets said. Learning your personal early signs, such as a hot face, clenched jaw, raised voice or a pulse you can feel, lets you call the time-out before you say the thing you will need to apologise for.

                ## Milestones
                1. A list of your own three to five physical signs that an argument is getting too hot.
                2. Your resting pulse noted, and the reading during a tense moment if you wear a tracker.
                3. Your partner's observations of your signs added, asked for kindly.
                4. A personal rule written down: when two signs appear, call the time-out.

                ## Notes
                Many people only notice flooding afterwards. Ask your partner which signs they see first; they often spot them before you do.
              priority: high
              deadlineOffsetDays: 21

            - name: Fair fighting rules for disagreements
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                A short list of rules agreed while calm is much easier to keep than good intentions in the heat of a row. Typical rules ban name-calling, swearing at each other, threats to leave, the words always and never, and dragging up old fights, and they give either partner something neutral to point at when a disagreement goes off course.

                ## Milestones
                1. Each partner's list of the behaviours they find hardest to recover from.
                2. A combined set of five to eight rules, each describing a behaviour rather than an outcome.
                3. A way to flag a broken rule mid-argument without it becoming a new fight.
                4. The rules written down somewhere both of you can see them.

                ## Notes
                Keep the list short enough to remember while upset. A rule like we stay on one topic works; a rule like we always resolve it tonight sets you up to fail.
              priority: high
              deadlineOffsetDays: 21
              frontmatter:
                mode: building
                output_kind: artifact
                success_criteria: "A list of five to eight fair fighting rules, agreed by both partners, is written down and kept somewhere visible."
                cadence: one-shot
                effort_hours_estimate: "1"
              tasks:
                - "List the three behaviours in arguments you find hardest to forgive"
                - "Merge your list with your partner's into five to eight rules"
                - "Agree a short phrase for flagging a broken rule mid-argument"
                - "Put the final rules on the fridge or in a shared note"
            - name: Shared repair phrase kit
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Repair attempts are the small bids that stop an argument escalating: a joke, a touch, I see your point, can we start again. They work only when the other person recognises and accepts them, so writing a list of phrases you would each welcome, and ones that would make things worse, gives you a vocabulary that lands even when you are both upset.

                ## Milestones
                1. A list of ten repair phrases or gestures, sorted into calming, apologising, agreeing and restarting.
                2. Each partner's three most welcome repairs starred.
                3. Any phrase that feels sarcastic or dismissive to one of you crossed out.
                4. The kit kept on both phones for a quick look mid-argument.

                ## Notes
                Humour is the most powerful and the riskiest repair. Agree which kinds of joke help and which feel like mockery.
              priority: high

            - name: Spotting criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Four behaviours, attacking character instead of a behaviour, contempt such as eye-rolling and mockery, defensiveness and shutting down, are the most reliable signs in couples research that conflict is doing damage. Learning what each looks like in your own home, and its usual antidote, gives you a shared language for what went wrong without blaming the person.

                ## Milestones
                1. A one-page summary of the four patterns, with an example of each from your own arguments.
                2. The antidote to each written beside it: gentle start-up, appreciation, taking responsibility, self-soothing.
                3. Each partner's most frequent pattern identified by themselves, not by the other.
                4. A month of weekly notes on which pattern showed up and whether the antidote was tried.

                ## Notes
                Use the language to spot your own patterns, not to diagnose your partner's. Saying that was contempt in the middle of a row is itself criticism.
              priority: medium
              frontmatter:

                4. Eight debriefs completed over a few months.

# Couple Conflict Repair

Every couple argues; what decides how the relationship fares is whether the arguments get repaired. This area starts with the foundations (mapping your recent fights, a time-out signal, knowing when you are flooded, fair fighting rules, a shared repair phrase kit and a safety check), then the operating rhythm that stops small hurts piling up, the skills of gentle start-ups, apologies and listening under heat, the changes and decisions that break stuck patterns, events such as the first days after a big blow-up, variants for parents, new babies, couples living apart or across cultures, and finally the advanced work of couples who already repair well.

What repeats is a weekly debrief of any real argument and a midweek clear-out of small irritations, a daily pause before raising something when tired or hungry, monthly time-out drills, conflict log entries and apology follow-through checks, a quarterly look at the arguments that keep returning and a yearly pattern review.
