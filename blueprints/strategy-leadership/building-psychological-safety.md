            - name: Team psychological safety baseline survey
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Before changing anything, you need a number you can come back to. Amy Edmondson's seven-item team survey, run anonymously with everyone in the team, gives a baseline on whether people feel able to raise problems, take risks and ask for help, and it takes about ten minutes to complete. Running it now means later changes are judged against evidence rather than the leader's impression, which is usually rosier than the team's.

                ## Milestones
                1. An anonymous survey built from the seven published team items, scored on a 1 to 7 agreement scale.
                2. At least 80 percent of the team responded, with a minimum of five responses before any result is shown.
                3. Item scores and the overall average recorded, with the two lowest items named.
                4. The results shared back with the team within two weeks of the survey closing.

                ## Notes
                Start from the **Metrics log** template to keep the scores. In a team smaller than five, anonymity breaks down and people answer cautiously, so lean on the observation and conversation projects instead.
              priority: high
              deadlineOffsetDays: 21
              frontmatter:
                mode: research
                output_kind: knowledge
                success_criteria: "Anonymous baseline scores for all seven items, from at least 80 percent of the team, are recorded and shared back within two weeks of closing."
                cadence: one-shot
                effort_hours_estimate: "4"
              tasks:
                - "Copy the seven published team psychological safety items into an anonymous form"
                - "Set a minimum of five responses before any result can be viewed"
                - "Send the survey with a two-line note on why you are asking and what happens next"
                - "Record the item scores in a metrics log and mark the two lowest"
                - "Share the results at the next team meeting, lowest scores first"
            - name: Leader's own speak-up signals self-audit
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Leaders rarely see the small moves that teach a team to stay quiet: the sigh at a late problem, checking a phone while a junior person talks, answering a question before it is finished. A week of noting your own reactions, plus a frank question to one trusted colleague, shows which habits do the most damage. It is also the cheapest change available, because nobody else has to agree to it.

                ## Milestones

                2. Five working days of brief notes on how you reacted when someone raised a problem or disagreed.
                3. One trusted peer asked what they see you do when challenged, with the answer written down.
                4. Two behaviours chosen to stop and one to start, each written as a specific action.
              priority: high
              deadlineOffsetDays: 14
              frontmatter:
                mode: research
                output_kind: knowledge
                success_criteria: "Two habits to stop and one to start, drawn from a week of self-notes and one peer's observation, are written down."
                cadence: one-shot
                effort_hours_estimate: "3"
              tasks:
                - "List ten things a leader does that make people go quiet or speak up"
                - "Rate yourself honestly against each item on the list"

                - "Ask a trusted peer what they see you do when someone challenges you"
                - "Write the two habits you will stop and the one you will start"

            - name: Bad news travel time audit
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Healthy teams surface problems early; fearful ones sit on them until they cannot be hidden. Tracing the last three problems that hit your team, from the day someone first suspected it to the day you heard, gives a concrete measure of how long bad news takes to travel. A gap of weeks usually points to a specific fear you can name and remove.

                ## Milestones
                1. Three recent problems chosen, such as a missed deadline, a defect or a lost customer.
                2. For each, the date someone first suspected it and the date it reached you, found through calm conversations.
                3. The reason for each delay recorded in the words of the people involved.
                4. An average travel time in days recorded as a baseline.

                ## Notes
                Say in every conversation that you are examining the system, not hunting for whoever kept quiet. If anyone suffers for an honest answer here, the audit has done more harm than good.
              priority: high
              deadlineOffsetDays: 30
              frontmatter:
                mode: research
                output_kind: knowledge
                success_criteria: "Three problems are traced with first-known and leader-heard dates, the reasons for delay recorded, and an average travel time written down."
                cadence: one-shot
                effort_hours_estimate: "5"
              tasks:
                - "Pick three problems from the last six months that reached you late"
                - "Ask the people involved when they first suspected each problem"
                - "Write down why each delay happened, in their words"
                - "Work out the average days between first knowing and you hearing"
                - "Share what you learned with the team without naming who waited"

# Building Psychological Safety

This area is for managers who want a team that tells them the truth early, and for team members who want to help build one. It starts with the foundations (a baseline survey, an honest look at your own reactions, the undiscussables and how long bad news takes to reach you), then the routines that keep voice alive week to week, the skills of asking, listening and disagreeing well, the decisions that remove hidden penalties for candour, the events worth preparing for, the situations that make safety harder, and finally the work of leaders who build it across several teams.

What repeats is a Friday reflection on how you handled bad news, a rotating challenger in decision meetings, a monthly mistake share and near-miss review, a follow-through check on every concern raised, a quarterly climate pulse and an annual reset.
