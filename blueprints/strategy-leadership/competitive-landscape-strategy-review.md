            - name: Framing the decision the competitive review must inform
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Competitor research without a decision attached grows into a folder nobody opens. Writing down the one or two choices the review must feed, such as which segment to prioritise next year or whether to match a rival's price cut, tells you what evidence matters and what can be ignored.

                ## Milestones
                1. One or two decisions written as questions, each with the person who will make it.
                2. The date by which each decision is needed agreed with that person.
                3. A short list of what the review will deliberately not cover.
                4. The framing shared with the sponsor and confirmed in writing.

                ## Notes
                A good test: if the review found something surprising, would anyone change a budget, a target or a roadmap? If not, the question is too vague.
              priority: high
              deadlineOffsetDays: 14
              frontmatter:
                mode: building

                cadence: one-shot
                effort_hours_estimate: "3"
              tasks:
                - "Write down the two decisions this review should help someone make"
                - "Ask the sponsor when each decision has to be taken"
                - "List the topics the review will leave out on purpose"

            - name: Drawing the boundary of the market you compete in
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Defining the market too narrowly hides the substitute that is quietly taking your customers; defining it too broadly makes every company a competitor. Setting the boundary by the customer need served, the segments you sell to and the geographies you cover gives the whole review a fixed frame.

                ## Milestones
                1. The customer need or job written in one plain sentence, without naming your product.
                2. Segments and geographies inside the boundary listed, with those outside named too.
                3. Four tiers of alternatives defined: direct rivals, adjacent players, substitutes and doing nothing.
                4. The boundary checked against five recent lost deals to see whether it explains them.
              priority: high
              deadlineOffsetDays: 21
              frontmatter:
                mode: research
                output_kind: knowledge
                success_criteria: "A written market definition states the customer need, segments, geographies and four tiers of alternatives, and accounts for at least four of the last five lost deals."
                cadence: one-shot
                effort_hours_estimate: "4"
              tasks:
                - "Write the customer need your offer meets without naming the product"
                - "List the segments and regions inside and outside your boundary"
                - "Sort known alternatives into direct, adjacent, substitute and do nothing"
                - "Test the boundary against your last five lost deals"

            - name: Rules for gathering competitor information ethically
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Competitive intelligence goes wrong when someone poses as a customer under false pretences, asks a new hire to bring a former employer's files, or swaps pricing notes with a rival at an event. A short set of sourcing rules, checked with your legal adviser, protects the company and keeps the evidence usable in front of the board.

                ## Milestones
                1. A one-page list of permitted sources, such as public filings, websites, published prices, customer conversations and trade shows.
                2. Prohibited practices written plainly, including misrepresentation and using confidential material from former employers.
                3. Guidance on contact with competitors at industry events agreed with your legal adviser.
                4. The rules shared with everyone who contributes to the competitive folder.

                ## Notes
                Competition law treats any exchange of pricing or capacity plans between rivals very seriously in most jurisdictions. Ask your legal adviser what applies to you, and record their guidance rather than interpreting the law yourself.

                ## Purpose
                Buyers weigh options in ways internal teams rarely see: a rival's free trial, a colleague's recommendation, or the safe choice of doing nothing. # Competitive Landscape Strategy Review

This area is for managers, founders and enterprise strategy teams who need an honest picture of the competition and want it to change decisions, not sit in a slide appendix. It starts with foundations (the question the review must answer, the market boundary, a long list of rivals, sourcing rules, profiles and your own baseline), then the routines that keep evidence flowing, the analytical skills that turn evidence into insight, the choices about where to compete, how to differentiate and where to stay out, the events that test the thinking, versions for founders, product managers, new leaders and multi-unit groups, and finally the specialist work of cost modelling, ecosystem mapping and running a competitive intelligence function.

What repeats is a Friday field-notes sweep, monthly signal digests, win and loss interviews, release, hiring and share tracking, a quarterly landscape review with pricing and battlecard refreshes, and a yearly full refresh of the map and the choices made from it.
