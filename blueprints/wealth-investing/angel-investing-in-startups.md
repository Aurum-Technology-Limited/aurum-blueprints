              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Most angel cheques return nothing, the winners can take eight to twelve years to pay out, and the shares cannot be sold early if your plans change. Before backing a single startup, an honest check of your emergency fund, pension progress, debts and appetite for total loss settles whether angel investing is a deliberate small slice of your wealth, a learning exercise through tiny cheques, or something to postpone.

                ## Milestones
                1. Emergency cash, high-interest debt and pension contributions confirmed as in order before any startup cheque.
                2. Your motives for angel investing listed, with the ones about excitement or status marked honestly.
                3. A written view on whether you could lose every penny invested without changing your retirement plans.
                4. A recorded yes, not yet or no, with the conditions that would change the answer.

                ## Notes
                Nothing in this area is a recommendation to invest. Startup shares are high risk and illiquid; if in doubt, talk to a regulated adviser who understands unlisted investments before committing money.

                Before you start: this plan is general information to help you organise your own money decisions. It is not financial, investment, tax or legal advice. Check decisions that matter with a qualified, licensed adviser in your country, and remember that investing and trading can lose money.

# Angel Investing in Startups

Angel investing is the riskiest and least liquid corner of a wealth plan, so this area is for founders and anyone else who wants to back startups with a ceiling, a portfolio plan and proper records rather than enthusiasm. It starts with the foundations (whether to do it at all, investor status, a ceiling, cheque sizing, tax reliefs, a document vault, a thesis, a first route, conflicts and payment safety), then the routines that run a portfolio, the skills of reading convertibles, term sheets, cap tables and startup accounts, decisions about follow-ons, syndicates, networks, platforms and funds, dated events such as a first close, a bridge round, a shutdown or an acquisition, variants for founders, busy employees, small budgets and households, and finally specialist roles such as leading a syndicate, sitting in on a board or scouting for a fund.

The rhythm is a weekly deal flow triage, monthly checks on deployment pace, open promises to founders and the network's pitch calendar, a quarterly read of investor updates and chase of relief certificates, and yearly valuation marks, ceiling checks and a portfolio review. The Operational checklist, Meeting notes, Metrics log, Purchase decision and Board meeting templates pair with the diligence checklist, first founder calls, valuation marks, the crowdfunding platform choice and an observer seat.
