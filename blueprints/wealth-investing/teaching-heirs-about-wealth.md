            - name: Family money values statement
              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Heirs who inherit money without knowing what it was for tend to fill the gap with guesses, and the guesses are rarely the ones you would choose. A one-page statement of what wealth is for in your family, such as security, education, freedom to choose work, generosity, gives every later lesson and conversation something to point back to. Writing it now, while you can explain it in your own words, is far easier than leaving heirs to reconstruct it from a will.

                ## Milestones
                1. Five to eight values about money listed by each parent or grandparent separately.
                2. The lists compared and merged into no more than five shared values.
                3. A one-page statement written in plain language a twelve-year-old could follow.
                4. The statement stored with the family papers and a copy given to each adult heir.

                ## Notes
                Keep it short and concrete. "We pay for education but not for cars" teaches more than "we value learning".

                Before you start: this plan is general information to help you organise your own money decisions. It is not financial, investment, tax or legal advice. Check decisions that matter with a qualified, licensed adviser in your country, and remember that investing and trading can lose money.

              description: |-
                ## Purpose
                Telling a nine-year-old the size of the estate is as unhelpful as letting a thirty-year-old find out from the solicitor. A disclosure ladder sets out what each heir learns at which age or life stage, from "we save and give" at seven to the outline of the estate plan at twenty-five. It removes the guesswork from every awkward question and makes sure nobody is surprised at the end.

                4. The ladder agreed with your partner and filed with the disclosure agreement.

                ## Notes
                Stages often matter more than birthdays. A financially steady twenty-two-year-old may be ready for more than an unsettled twenty-eight-year-old.

# Teaching Heirs About Wealth

This area is for parents and grandparents who want the money they leave to help the next generation rather than overwhelm it. It starts with foundations (your family's values, what each heir already knows and an agreed plan for what they learn when), moves through the routines that teach by doing, from pocket money jars to quarterly family meetings, then covers skills lessons, the harder decisions about bailouts and conditions, the milestone conversations, situations such as blended families or an heir in trouble, and finally the structures larger families use across several generations.

The rhythm is weekly for young children's pocket money, monthly for money talks, teen budgets and mentoring, quarterly for family meetings and development check-ins, and yearly for the family assembly, the giving budget and your private review of each heir's readiness. The Skills matrix, Reading queue, Meeting notes, Monthly budget and bills and Development plan templates pair well with it.
